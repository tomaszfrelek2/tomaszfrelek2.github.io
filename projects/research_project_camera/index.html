Impact of Camera Defects on Detection

A comprehensive study on the resilience of YOLOv11 against sensor degradation and the efficacy of adversarial training.

Paper, data, and code are available at my github.

Introduction

Autonomous driving systems are increasingly reliant on camera-based perception to navigate complex environments. While some manufacturers, such as Tesla, rely primarily on camera suites to guide their vehicles, the long-term reliability of these sensors remains an under-researched area. Unlike mechanical components that undergo regular maintenance, camera sensor suites are often unregulated and remain in use well beyond their initial calibration period.

Over time, these sensors are exposed to environmental stressors—heat, vibration, and radiation—that lead to physical and digital degradation. This study categorizes these defects into two types:

  • Physical Defects: Issues arising from hardware damage, such as lens scratches, focus drift, or contamination from mud and debris.
  • Digital Defects: Artifacts stemming from internal processing or sensor aging, such as dead pixels, broken scanlines, or sensor blooming.

As autonomous fleets age, the likelihood of these defects compromising safety-critical object detection systems increases. This project utilizes the YOLOv11 architecture to benchmark detection performance against these defects and proposes adversarial training as a viable mitigation strategy.

Implementation Details

To systematically evaluate robustness, we designed an experiment that introduces simulated defects into a standard autonomous driving dataset.

1. Dataset and Model We utilized the Udacity Self-Driving Car Dataset, which contains 15,000 images capturing diverse driving scenarios in the San Francisco Bay area. The model selected was YOLOv11s (small), a lightweight version with 9.4M parameters, chosen for its balance of speed and accuracy. The model was pre-trained on COCO and fine-tuned on the Udacity dataset.

2. Training Configuration All training was conducted using NVIDIA Tesla T4 GPUs.

  • Epochs: 10
  • Batch Size: 16
  • Optimizer: Adam (Initial Learning Rate: 0.001)
  • Image Size: 512x512 pixels

3. Defect Simulation Pipeline Defects were digitally synthesized and overlaid on the test set to mimic real-world degradation:

  • Mud/Dirt: Generated by overlaying irregular, blurred brown polygons with adjustable opacity to simulate partial occlusion.
  • Cracks: Alpha-blended crack images were resized and overlaid to introduce linear obstruction patterns.
  • Chromatic Aberration: Simulated by shifting the Red and Blue color channels in opposite directions to create color fringing.
  • Broken Scanlines: Horizontal black lines were drawn across images to mimic CMOS sensor readout failures.
  • Sensor Blooming: Bright regions were detected and subjected to Gaussian blur to simulate charge overflow from high-intensity light sources.

Discussion and Results

The evaluation revealed that the impact of camera defects is non-uniform across different object classes and defect types.

Vulnerability of Small Objects

Small, detail-dependent classes were disproportionately affected. While the baseline model achieved a 0.803 mAP@50 for Red Traffic Lights, introducing chromatic aberration caused this to drop to 0.412. This suggests that color-dependent objects are highly sensitive to channel misalignments. Similarly, pedestrian detection proved fragile; in the presence of mud, pedestrian mAP@50 fell from 0.629 to 0.394.

Defect Severity Analysis

  • High Impact (Mud/Dirt): Mud caused the most significant performance drop, with car detection recall falling to 0.547. This is likely because mud splatters occlude critical features (edges and textures) that the model relies on[cite: 213].
  • Low Impact (Blooming): Sensor blooming had minimal impact on this dataset. We posit this is because the dataset was collected during the day; blooming mostly affected the sky rather than ground-level objects.
  • Compound Effects: When multiple defects were applied simultaneously, the system failed catastrophically. Pedestrian mAP@50 plummeted to 0.164, with a recall of only 0.174, indicating the model missed over 80% of pedestrians.

Adversarial Training as a Remedy

To address these failures, we trained a secondary YOLOv11s model using adversarial training, where 50% of the training set was augmented with simulated mud splatters.

The results were highly promising:

  1. Restored Robustness: On the mud-defected test set, the adversarially trained model improved car mAP@50 by 24.5% and pedestrian mAP@50 by 16% compared to the baseline.
  2. Maintained Baseline Accuracy: Crucially, when tested on clean, unmodified images, the robust model performed almost identically to the original model.
Left: The effects of multiple defects on object detection. Right: Comparison of the Baseline Model vs. the Adversarially Trained model on the Mud Test Set. Top row is base model, middle row is adversarially trained model, bottom is ground truth.

This study confirms that while modern detectors like YOLOv11 are efficient, they are brittle to sensor degradation. However, by incorporating defect-augmented data into the training pipeline, we can harden these systems against real-world wear and tear without sacrificing standard performance.
